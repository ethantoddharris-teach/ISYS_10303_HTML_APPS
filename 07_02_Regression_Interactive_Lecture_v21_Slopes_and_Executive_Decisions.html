Cryptid Coffee · Research to Revenue

Maya has a costly operational decision and 60 weekends of evidence. Investigate what was measured, decide how to analyze the question, and discover what the data can—and cannot—tell her.

Cryptid Coffee is an unconventional mobile coffee company whose pop-up shops draw crowds with towering inflatable cryptid mascots. During concerts, campus events, mall promotions and weekend festivals, the team installs an inflatable character beside its espresso trailer. A six- to eight-foot display is inexpensive and portable, but a massive twenty-foot cryptid can be seen across a parking lot. Operations director Maya Chen wants to know whether paying to transport and assemble taller mascots is actually worthwhile, or whether the association she sees on sales dashboards is misleading.

The analytics team assembled data from 60 operating weekends, with 20 observations in each of three venue types: Mall, Campus and Festival. Each row records the mascot's height in feet, the venue category, estimated foot traffic in hundreds of visitors, and actual weekend revenue in thousands of dollars. The first question is deliberately narrow: across these weekends, do taller mascots tend to be associated with higher average revenue? Maya wants to know how reliable that pattern is before making a budget decision.

There is a complication. Festivals tend to attract larger crowds and use larger displays, while mall sites typically have smaller mascots and different customer traffic. Venue managers chose display sizes and the sites at which they operated; nobody randomly assigned heights to comparable locations. The inflatables were installed before each weekend began, and revenue was recorded afterward. A positive overall relationship might therefore reflect where the mascots operate rather than what changing height would do. Maya knows that looking at the overall pattern could hide differences between locations. She wants the analytics team to investigate what changes when the venue and local customer traffic are accounted for, and whether the additional complexity is worth it.

Before looking at the statistical results, Maya asks the analysts to begin with the possibility that mascot height has no linear relationship with expected weekend revenue. She wants to consider evidence for a relationship in either direction. A false alarm could lead to costly inflatables that do not improve sales; missing a real association could mean overlooking a useful forecasting signal. The team will use a 5% significance level for its first decision, while exploring how a different threshold could change the statistical conclusion.

Maya needs more than a statistically significant number. Before approving a costly new mascot fleet, she wants to know whether the evidence is convincing, how large any association seems to be, how uncertain the estimate is, and whether it could be useful for estimating revenue at heights already seen in the data. She also needs to know whether the study justifies changing mascot sizes or whether the company should first run a controlled pilot.
