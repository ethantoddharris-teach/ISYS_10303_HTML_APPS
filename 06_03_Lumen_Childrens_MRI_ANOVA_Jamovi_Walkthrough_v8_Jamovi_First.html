A decision from pediatric imaging

The scanner is ready. The child isn't.

At 8:35 on a busy Tuesday morning, a seven-year-old at Lumen Children’s Hospital is standing outside the MRI suite, clutching a stuffed fox. Her parents have been told the scan won't hurt, but the noises, the narrow tunnel, and the request to lie perfectly still have made it hard for her to take the next step. A nurse stays with the family and patiently talks through what will happen. In the waiting area, more children and caregivers are arriving for their appointments.

The imaging team doesn't want to rush frightened children or ask nurses to spend less time reassuring them. Instead, it is exploring whether better preparation could help families feel ready sooner. Standard Orientation uses a nurse-led explanation of the scanner and what to expect. Tablet Story lets the child work through an illustrated, narrated version of the experience. VR Rehearsal uses a headset to introduce the scanner room and its sounds before the actual visit to the machine. Each approach makes sense to someone on the team. The question is whether any of them meaningfully changes how long preparation takes.

Across the pilot, the team enrolled children ages 6–10 arriving for their first outpatient MRI who were eligible for any of the three non-drug preparation approaches. Children were randomly assigned to one program, and each child experienced that approach only once. The final cleaned analysis contains 150 complete observations—50 children in each group. Staff recorded the number of minutes between arrival in the imaging unit and being ready to enter the scanner; the spreadsheet calls this Prep_Time_Min. Lower values mean a shorter preparation period, not necessarily a better scan or a happier child. The raw scheduling export is available in the walkthrough, so the data need to be screened before the team trusts its summaries.

Message from Dr. Rao · Pediatric Imaging

“The nurses are hearing that children like the rehearsal. That matters, but it isn't enough for the budget meeting. Tell me whether the programs appear to differ on preparation time, which comparisons actually have evidence behind them, and how sure we can be.”

In a preliminary quality review, the clinical team reported reasonably symmetric preparation times without extreme outliers in each program, although individual preparation times varied considerably. The analysis will use a significance level of α = .05, but the finance director has a second question: The tablet program uses devices already owned by the unit and only requires a modest content update. VR requires new headsets, sanitation supplies, additional staff training, and a careful check of which children tolerate the headset. The finance director says any VR purchase would need convincing evidence of at least six minutes of average savings compared with Standard Orientation—and asks whether VR does anything meaningfully better than the simpler tablet program.

Your recommendation must distinguish a statistical difference from a large-enough operational benefit. The pilot cannot answer questions about scan quality, sedation, or every child seen by the hospital.
